This note maps the redesigned Instagram set against Hawaii's public adjusting
law (HRS chapter 431, article 9, including the public adjuster provisions
Hawaii adopted in 2020 via SB2876, which follow the NAIC Public Adjuster
Licensing Model Act) and the Insurance Code's advertising rules. It is a
marketing-compliance checklist, not legal advice — have coverage counsel do a
final read before publishing.

## What the law requires (and how the posts handle it)

**1. Licensed status and identification — HRS §431:9-201 (license required to
act as an adjuster) and the contract provisions adopted from the NAIC model
(public adjuster's full name, business address, and license number on record
with the Insurance Division must appear in the written contract).**
Every tile and both videos carry a persistent footer:
`HI Public Adjuster License #19246735 · Hawaiʻi DCCA Insurance Division · No recovery, no fee`.
The license number matches the one displayed on kuleanaclaims.com. Advertising
under the exact licensed identity keeps the feed consistent with the contract
documents a client will later sign.

**2. No misrepresentation — HRS §431:13-102 (misrepresentation and false
advertising of policy contracts) and the adjuster discipline provisions of
article 9 (misrepresentation as grounds for license action).**
- No post promises an outcome, a settlement amount, or a percentage increase.
  Copy is limited to describing services ("we document, itemize, and pursue
  every dollar your policy actually owes") and verifiable process facts.
- The denied-claims tile says "**Many** denied claims can be re-opened and
  challenged" — deliberately not "most" or "all," and framed as possibility,
  not guarantee.
- Recreated damage imagery is disclosed as an **illustrative recreation in
  the caption of every post that uses it** (on-image stamps were removed at
  the owner's request; the caption disclosure is the compliance control —
  do not drop it). Posts 11 and 21 use photos from the owner's own library
  rather than AI imagery; confirm licensing for those before wide use. No
  post presents any image as an actual client property or claim file.

**3. Fee representations — Hawaii's adopted NAIC-model fee provisions
(compensation terms must be stated in the written contract; if the carrier
pays or commits to policy limits within 72 hours of the loss report, the
public adjuster is limited to reasonable time-based compensation).**
The posts state only "No recovery, no fee" — a truthful description of
contingency work — and never quote a percentage. Fee percentages belong in
the written contract, where the statute requires them. Nothing in the copy
conflicts with the 72-hour limitation because no post promises a fee
arrangement to any specific claimant.

**4. No unauthorized practice of law.**
Copy describing disputed claims points to negotiation, the policy's appraisal
clause, and referral to coverage counsel (mirroring the FAQ on
kuleanaclaims.com) — the posts never offer legal representation or legal
advice.

**5. Financial-interest and referral disclosures (adopted NAIC-model
standards of conduct).**
No post refers followers to any repair contractor or vendor, so no
referral-disclosure obligation is triggered by this content.

**6. General advertising hygiene.**
- The claimed credential ("licensed public adjuster") is accurate and
  verifiable through the DCCA Insurance Division licensee search.
- "Free inspection" / "free policy review" statements match the services
  actually offered free of charge on kuleanaclaims.com.
- The "carrier's adjuster works for the carrier" framing states the parties'
  roles factually ("two different adjusters, two different clients") without
  disparaging any named insurer — consistent with §431:13-102's defamation
  and false-statement prohibitions.

## Things to keep doing when writing future captions

1. Always include the license number line (it is baked into every template).
2. Never promise outcomes, amounts, or timeframes; describe process.
3. Keep the "(Imagery/Footage is an illustrative recreation.)" caption line
   on any staged or AI-generated damage imagery; never present it as a real
   client claim. If a real claim photo is used, get the client's written
   permission first and never show claim numbers, addresses, or policy
   details.
4. Don't quote fee percentages in ads — those belong in the written contract.
5. In-person and loss-site solicitation is regulated more tightly than
   advertising; this set is feed content, not door-to-door material.
