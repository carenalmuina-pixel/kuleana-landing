# Florida & Hawaii Public-Adjuster Advertising Compliance

Caren Almuina holds two active public-adjuster licenses used for regulated marketing:
**Florida** — Lic. #W583930 (JLAC Corp, subcontractor to Monarch Claims Consultants, `@yenclaims`)
**Hawaii** — Lic. #19246735 (Kuleana Claims LLC, `@kuleanaclaims`)

Both states can fine unlicensed or non-compliant public-adjuster advertising — Hawaii $1,000–$10,000 per violation, Florida $500–$5,000 per violation (Florida DFS, under §626.9541). This skill exists because that penalty is real and because the two businesses already run **automated, scheduled posting pipelines** (Buffer, Postiz) — content can go out with nobody reading it in the moment it publishes. The checklist has to be applied before scheduling, not after something goes live.

## The two rulesets differ — don't cross-apply them

The states genuinely diverge on real points, confirmed via fresh research (2026-08-05, not carried over from training data — public-adjuster advertising rules are exactly the kind of thing that changes and gets litigated):

- **Florida had a 48-hour post-catastrophe solicitation ban. It's gone.** Struck down as unconstitutional by the Florida Supreme Court in 2012 (*Atlas Adjustment Bureau v. Dep't of Fin. Servs.* line of cases). If you see this "48-hour ban" claim anywhere referencing Florida — including in older notes in this account — it's wrong; don't repeat it. Florida's actual live restriction is different: solicitation is limited to Monday–Saturday, 8 a.m.–8 p.m. (§626.854), not a post-event blackout window.
- **Hawaii has no equivalent solicitation-hours restriction** in what's been found so far — its core advertising rules center on license/identification, misrepresentation, and fee-representation timing tied to the insurer's 72-hour payment window (see the Hawaii reference file). Don't assume Florida's Monday–Saturday/8–8 rule applies there.
- **Penalty ranges differ**: Hawaii $1,000–$10,000/violation; Florida $500–$5,000/violation. Don't quote one state's number for the other.

## Shared checklist logic (applies to both, details differ — see reference files)

- **Identification.** Full name + license number, matching what's on file with the regulator, on every piece of marketing material — not just the website, every social asset too.
- **No misrepresentation.** Never promise an outcome, a dollar amount, a percentage, or a timeframe. Describe the *process*, not the *result*. Hedge with "many," not "most" or "all." A denial is "a position," not "a verdict."
- **Imagery honesty.** Disclose staged, recreated, stock, or AI-generated imagery in the caption/copy itself (not just an on-image watermark — those get cropped off in reposts). Never present synthetic imagery as a real client's property. Real client photos need written permission and have identifying details (claim numbers, addresses, exact dates) stripped.
- **Fee representation.** Describe the fee *model* ("no recovery, no fee," etc.) without quoting percentages or dollar figures — those belong in the signed contract, not the ad, in both states.
- **No unauthorized practice of law.** Route disputed-claim questions to negotiation, the policy's appraisal clause, or referral to coverage counsel — never present as legal advice or representation.
- **Timing sensitivity.** Even without Florida's old 48-hour rule, err toward pausing urgency-driven CTAs (not educational content) immediately after a live declared disaster — the underlying "don't look like you're chasing traumatized homeowners" concern that motivated the old rule is still a real reputational and regulatory-attention risk even though the specific statute is gone.
