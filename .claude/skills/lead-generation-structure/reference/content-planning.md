# Content-Planning Method

A repeatable way to generate a content backlog instead of relying on one-off
inspiration. Cross-reference three inputs into a single topic map, then keep
the map alive as a running backlog rather than a one-time brainstorm.

## The three inputs

1. **What the product or service actually covers or solves.** Enumerate the
   real scenarios, problems, or use-cases the business handles — for an
   insurance-claims business, the perils a policy covers; for a law
   practice, its case types; for a contractor, its project types; for a
   software product, the jobs-to-be-done it addresses. This is the raw topic
   inventory everything else gets checked against. Be exhaustive here even
   if not every topic gets used immediately — gaps against this list are
   what step 3 finds.

2. **What established competitors publish.** Look at competitor content
   (social feeds, sites, ads) and sort it into recurring pattern *types*
   rather than copying specific posts — these pattern types show up across
   almost every industry:
   - **Education** — "what this problem looks like / what it becomes if
     ignored." Usually the single most common and highest-performing
     format.
   - **Process explainers** — what the business actually does, step by
     step, and who the other parties in the transaction really work for.
   - **Literacy content** — teach the customer the thing competitors hope
     they never learn (how the fine print works, how the fee actually gets
     calculated, what the contract really says). Firms that own this niche
     build outsized trust.
   - **Story arcs** — before/during/after narratives, only ever with a real
     customer's informed, written consent.
   - **Timely/seasonal posts** — tied to a recurring calendar event
     relevant to the niche (storm season, tax season, open enrollment,
     back-to-school).
   - **Credential/trust posts** — licensing, "how we get paid," regulator
     links, team introductions. Usually under-used by competitors, so it's
     an easy differentiator.
   - **What NOT to do** — an explicit exclusion list of competitor patterns
     that are compliance liabilities or just read as low-trust (outcome
     guarantees, flashing a settlement check, before/after photos without
     consent). Naming these up front stops a later draft from copying the
     wrong lesson from a competitor's feed.

3. **Gaps in the current content.** Compare what's live today against the
   full inventory from input 1. The delta is the actual backlog — and it
   usually reveals higher-value topics than either "more of what already
   works" or "whatever's top of mind this week."

## Output format

A topic map table, one row per topic:

| Topic | What it looks like (the concrete hook/visual) | Asset | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| *(from input 1)* | *(the specific, vivid detail that makes it a post, not a category)* | *(file/link if produced)* | ready / in progress / idea |

Keep a **prioritized shortlist** of the next batch at the top or bottom of
the map — rank it by topics that are simultaneously under-served by
competitors (input 2) and clearly needed by the audience (input 1), since
those are the highest-leverage next pieces to produce.

## Keeping it alive

Treat the map as a living backlog, not a one-time planning document — revisit
it whenever a new competitor pattern shows up, a new product/service line
launches, or the gap analysis in step 3 gets stale. A content-planning
document nobody reopens after the first week is worth nothing.
